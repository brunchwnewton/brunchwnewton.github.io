One quick and easy application of some of these principles are as pertains to the pacing of our Golden Trek.

There are essentially two types of Departure Points: an internal, and an external. An internal Departure Point is one which is brought about by the actions or lack thereof of factors within a Golden Trek's homeworld itself. For instance, nuclear war or bioweapons in all their unleashed horror could be potential internal Departure Points. As for external Departure Points, they are something of a constant threat from outside the center of the Golden Trek as opposed to inside. The main reason they should be categorized individually is because these external Departure Points are roughly constant in magnitude of threat, because after all the universe is roughly constant in makeup. A planet-killing asteroid is an example of an external Departure Point, as is a nearby supernova or even something more exotic like a strangelet, if they even exist. Perhaps the most common of these external Departure Points is simple orbital chaos, triggered by the behemoths of gravitation that are passing stars. The fact that Earth has not been uprooted from the goldilocks zone by this type of disaster thus far is nothing short of Miraculous, and hence the external Departure Point threat is increased by it. Now, there are always variations here and there, but it is nonetheless safe to say that over large timescales, the instantaneous threat of the external Departure Point is roughly constant due to roughly constant cosmic phenomena.

This means that given some length of time t, and an external Departure Point hazard function value of λ, the likelihood of the Earth surviving is e−λt.

From here, one might consider the prospect of squeezing the Golden Trek to a shorter time frame, altogether shaving off time t from its end. If our Golden Trek wrapped up and triggered the Golden Point t time earlier than it did in actuality, then, due to the nature of hazard functions, we would be precisely eλt times more likely to not undergo an external Departure Point. Because our future will someday have to see either a Golden Point or a Departure Point, it follows that G + D = 1, where G and D are the total likelihoods of the Golden Point and Departure Point respectively. Also, because the Departure Point can be further subdivided precisely into the internal Departure Probability, I, and external Departure Probability, E, the fundamental relation must be so.

G + I + E = 1

Defining the Earth's values for these quantities as G₀ + I₀ + E₀ = 1, their being measured with respect to some arbitrary very distant past reference point, and also defining G₁ + I₁ + E₁ = 1 as the values for the shortened Golden Trek allows for manipulation to be possible. To start, because the likelihood of no external Departure Point taking place increases by a factor of eλt due to hazard function logic, it also follows that (1−E₁)/(1−E₀) = eλt, or likewise G₁ + I₁ = eλt(G₀ + I₀). Solving for G₁ in this case yields G₁ = eλtG₀ + eλtI₀ − I₁. By the Third Law, b/a < p/(1−p), and also in this case, the Outlook Probability p of our Golden Trek occurring as opposed to the shortened one is p = a/(a+b), because it simply represents the likelihood of our Golden Point occurring rather than the alternative Golden Point, a concept which itself is equal to the likelihood of ours out of the total likelihood of either. Together, this means that it must be the case for b < a, and so G₁ < G₀. Substitution yields eλtG₀ + eλtI₀ − I₁ < G₀, and then further algebraic manipulation returns the result.

I₁/I₀ > eλt [1 + (G₀/I₀)(1 − eλt)]

Because t must be a positive number to represent a "positive" amount of time in the past, the quantity of (G₀/I₀)(1−eλt) must also be a positive number, meaning 1 + (G₀/I₀)(1−eλt) > 1 and thus I₁/I₀ > eλt consistently. Therefore, according to this logic, the more time t for which the Golden Trek is shortened, the higher the internal Departure Point probability will grow, and in an exponential fashion at that.

This is yet another example of where our Golden Trek is highly Optimal. If the Golden Trek were longer, the external Departure Point likelihood would increase exponentially, and if the Golden Trek were shorter, the internal Departure Point likelihood would increase exponentially. In effect, this situates the actual temporal locations of things in our past to align in the exact perfect spot to fight off these two combating forces. If the background hazard function of the external Departure Point were lower, this most optimal balancing point would move forward in time, because waiting slightly longer to perfect internal probabilities would be met with a less harsh external Departure Point likelihood in the process, since after all it would be slower to increase. Also the falloff of nudging the Golden Point in either temporal direction would be more gradual — or at least the lower bound of what is allowed would be. The rest of all this is essentially a kind of pacing process of situating events in the Golden Trek. The higher the background hazard function of the external Departure Point, the more pacing is required, and the more events must hasten to avoid wasting time. If there were zero threat of the external Departure Point, then hypothetically the events of our Golden Trek would have no time constraint at all, and could take endless time to complete tasks. But because there is an actual threat of the external Departure Point, the events of our past and Golden Trek must quicken accordingly — but not too far, might they have too much cost with respect to benefit, hence feeding the out-of-control internal Departure Point danger existing too far in that direction. What is truly feasible, then, is the middle ground, where both factors are relatively at their lowest potential — and this is just where one might find the Earth.
